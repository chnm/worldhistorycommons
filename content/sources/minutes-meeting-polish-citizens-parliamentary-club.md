Poland's first semi-free elections in early June 1989 indicated Poles' strongly anti-Communist and pro-Solidarity sentiments, as evidenced by the solid defeat of Communism. Following this historic election, the newly elected pro-Solidarity parliamentary leaders formed the Citizens' Parliamentary Club, in which they debated about the future of Poland's political system. At a meeting of this group on August 1, 1989, members discussed (and disagreed on) who should become the next prime minister as well as whether or not the group should form a coalition with another political party. In particular, they focused their attention on General Czeslaw Kiszczak, a long-time Communist Party leader and army officer; Kiszczak eventually served as prime minister for a couple of weeks in August 1989, after which he was succeeded by Tadeusz Mazowiecki, a Solidarity activist, who became the first non-Communist prime minister in Eastern Europe since the 1940s. As this document so clearly reveals, the process of creating a new form of governing in a time of uncertainty was fraught with tension, compromise, and swift change.

Minutes of the Meeting of the Presidium of the Citizens’ Parliamentary Club

1 August 1989, 8 p.m.

Present: J. Kuron, K. Kozlowski, A. Stelmachowski, Z. Kuratowska, T. Mazowiecki, B.<br>
Geremek, J. Ambroziak, A. Wielowieyski, H. Wujec, A. Balazs, J.M. Rokita, O.<br>
Krzyzanowska, J. Slisz, J. Ziolkowski, A. Michnik, E. Wende.

B. Geremek: I will remind you of the things that have taken place within the last<br>
few days and hours. I had a meeting with Gen. Kiszczak at 2 p.m. It turns out that, at a<br>
Politburo meeting, out of four candidates submitted for the position of prime minister<br>
only one is left—Rakowski. Baka and Malinowski have declined. Kiszczak is not willing<br>
either, but he thinks it’s his duty. He asked about the position of our Club. The Club<br>
decided to vote against [him] or to abstain. Walesa took the position: “I supported Gen.<br>
Kiszczak for president of the Polish People’s Republic, I refuse to support him for prime<br>
minister.” He asked me to inform the OKP about it.

Kiszczak had a very difficult meeting with the PUWP Club yesterday, when it<br>
was deciding about the discipline [in party line bloc] voting. Today only 120 members<br>
showed up, which means that 50 have deserted [the PUWP Club].

From the other Clubs the figures are changing. At one point, half of the ZSL and<br>
half of SD were against. Today it’s even worse—the whole ZSL is against [him], and<br>
from the SD only 4 persons [are in favor of him]. He lacks 80- 70 [sic] people to ensure<br>
his [Kiszczak’s] election.

Meetings of all three Clubs are going on, debating separately. The leaders have<br>
arrived, debates are stormy.

ZSL has come up with a proposal to form a government with the OKP. They think<br>
that the opposition should form the government. Bentkowski argues that the ZSL is<br>
decidedly against the candidacy of Kiszczak. He has contacts with the PUWP—there is a<br>
group of young PUWP parliamentary delegates who would like to meet jointly with [me],<br>
B. Geremek. If I meet with them, it would be an attempt to interfere with the coalition.<br>
They have to ask for it themselves.

Today it is to be decided whether General Jaruzelski will withdraw the letter<br>
proposing Kiszczak [for Prime Minister].

Bentkowski says [ZSL] cannot form a government with the PUWP. They are<br>
ready to do it with us.

[ZSL] is asking if we would leave the three main ministries with the PUWP if we<br>
were to form a government. This is an indispensable guarantee of a peaceful transfer of<br>
power.

When PUWP proposed a coalition with them, ZSL was offered 4 ministries and a<br>
vice premier. They were not expecting this from the opposition. They put forward their<br>
proposal not for the sake of bargaining, but because there is no other way out of the crisis<br>
in the country. If we would recognize this, they [ZSL] would be satisfied with 2<br>
ministries. At 6 p.m. there was a meeting with Orzechowski. Based on that conversation,<br>
the situation is at a critical point, the President’s motion is suspended.

On the other hand Bentkowski was still presenting doubts as to whether to enter<br>
into coalition with the PUWP. I admitted he was right—we know what cooperation with<br>
the PUWP did to the ZSL. They didn’t perceive it as arrogance. To be sure, after that<br>
conversation Jacek Kuron critically summed it up for me: we will take power if PUWP<br>
makes better conditions in the country for us.

We have to take into consideration quite unexpected solutions. Our whole Club is<br>
opposed, and yet they have to have a majority.

If Kiszczak won’t get through, then [perhaps] another candidate—Sekula. Club<br>
meetings are stormy, sharp with mutual accusations. Party leaders are convincing their<br>
Clubs to [decide in favor of] the coalition with PUWP.

We may very well dream that this is a parliamentary democracy and that the<br>
majority decides. But the dream may be cut off and reality will let us know where we are.<br>
We have to see the situation clearly.

J. Kuron: Is it true that the Senate has issued some sort of statement relating to the<br>
annulment of the President’s election?

A. Stelmachowski: Such motion has come in from Senator Leszek Piotrowski—I<br>
sent it out to the proper commission.

A. Wielowieyski: What is the motive of those 41 PUWP [members] who have not<br>
come to the meeting with Kiszczak?

J. Slisz: I spoke with Bak—a peasant, for them a membership card is not<br>
important, they want Bronislaw [Geremek]. As far as Bentkowski is concerned, they<br>
would like to have Olesiak in the government. Approximately 40 deputies are not going<br>
to vote for Kiszczak.

A. Balazs: Bentkowski said that Sekula’s candidacy also won’t get through.

J. Slisz: From a talk with Switka—we would have support of SD deputies.

J. Ambroziak: As of 8 p.m. the information is as follows:<br>
PUWP—12 deputies are against Kiszczak<br>
ZSL—60 deputies are against [Kiszczak]<br>
SD—the whole is in favor [of Kiszczak for Prime Minister].<br>
Pax, UChS—in favor [of Kiszczak for Prime Minister].

A. Michnik: Will the Club be in favor of not being involved in it?

J. Slisz: The ZSL was asking if we would be ready to propose a prime minister.<br>
We need to think about this.

A. Balazs - If we put forward our candidate for prime minister, the whole ZSL<br>
will be for him.

A. Stelmachowski: To sum it up, the situation is as follows:<br>
- some consensus is emerging to vote against Kiszczak.<br>
- are we to vote negatively against each PUWP candidate?<br>
- do we see the possibility of forming our own government with small<br>
concessions?

T. Mazowiecki: My position is known to all of you. When I was invited to the<br>
Council, I went, putting aside any other considerations. Since the moment I have learned<br>
about Kiszczak’s candidacy, I have been trying to form an opinion on this matter.

- I think that the Club’s decision to vote against Kiszczak is not good. I do not<br>
share the position of our Chairman, who is sending out this news by telex. SIS 162<br>
communicated this news yesterday evening.

- My political assessment is the following: if such a strong man is being proposed,<br>
then the power is being shifted towards the line of the parliament-government. It’s going<br>
to be a strong government, a situation will emerge, which will stabilize the process which<br>
has already begun. There is no need for the Club to vote against, it may abstain. I am<br>
afraid that the situation with the national list may repeat itself—first we are booming<br>
radicals, but then we withdraw. If we are not reaching for power ourselves, we should<br>
permit the other side to do it.

- As far as the ZSL proposal is concerned, one ought to remember that the ZSL<br>
doesn’t have access to the proper centers of power. I would not bet on this combination.<br>
There are other centers of power, which will let themselves be known. We are not at a<br>
stage, at which parliamentary relations decide.

I am opposed to Adam’s concept also for the reason that on the oppositionSolidarity side there is no program and within three months that would become<br>
dramatically clear.

I think that the most proper position on the question of prime minister is a neutral<br>
one. But if we were faced with a situation of the state crisis, then some talks about a great<br>
coalition might be possible, but not us in coalition with the ZSL.

I think that the moment is very serious. The public would not tolerate a situation<br>
in which first they see advances, and then withdrawals.

A. Celinski: […] I exclude the possibility of a great coalition.

The nearest option is something that took place in Spain—a government stands<br>
somewhere aside, it gains support from the ZSL, part of the Party, our Club can be<br>
convinced.

J. M. Rokita: I get the impression that a Kiszczak government, after all, would not<br>
be strong in a situation where it wouldn’t have support of a strong majority in the Sejm.<br>
It would be a government in which we would constantly have to be hypocrites. In<br>
the long run it would be a trap for us.<br>
Coalition with the ZSL is absurd. It would mean a clash of opinions from the<br>
beginning—that reforms are being introduced with a strong power center, the PUWP.<br>
Technically such coalition cannot be realized in defiance of the power centers.<br>
In case there is a government of a purely communist coalition, the reforms will be<br>
coming from them, they will be throwing them upon us, but they will not strike at the<br>
system, as markets would do. They will be lumping together various ideas and we would<br>
think there is no other alternative. It will be a consolidation of the system.<br>
It is necessary that we have at least part of the political initiatives. Something that<br>
is called a great coalition is a matter of time. It will come, it may be delayed, or<br>
accelerated. So, we should not be confusing people.

E. Wende: (to Mazowiecki) Do you take into consid-eration a situation in which<br>
the President will not recom-mend Kiszczak but Geremek?

T. Mazowiecki: It is possible, but we don’t have such a situation. At this moment<br>
there are back-corner talks with the ZSL.

There are two ways out:

A better one—a Kiszczak government, the strongest one from the other side. A<br>
big offensive, execution of legal reforms, great stability.

The second one—a great coalition with the PUWP.

A. Balazs: It’s a pity that such a discussion was not held prior to the presidential<br>
election. The situation that arose was the fault of both the Presidium and the Club. It<br>
would be very unfortunate if it were to repeat itself.

We have no chance for a coalition government, it would be short-lived and tragic<br>
for us because of the economic situation and the fact that we don’t have the people.

But the opposition certainly has a candidate for prime minister, as people from<br>
other parties see it. There are also people on the other side whom we might be able to put<br>
forward, e.g. Kwasniewski.

A. Wielowieyski: Two arguments can be added against the coalition:

- We should not be wasting our social capital by entering into a small coalition. I<br>
see no gain from it.<br>
- The Big Brother has other methods of conducting politics. Depriving the PUWP<br>
of power would be a blow to Gorbachev. The result—a mortal poisoning of our life,<br>
impossibility of realizing anything.<br>
It is apparent that we will have to support one government or the other. We must<br>
get them to understand that another candidate would get our support. Though Kiszczak is<br>
not bad.

[Break]

J. Ziolkowski: We are observing a great acceleration of the political process.<br>
Pacta sunt servanda—this has been our principle. The fact that Jaruzelski is president is<br>
good, it is a stabilizing factor. There is a great weakness of power, a rebellion with the<br>
Party itself. There is a dissention within the coalition, the ZSL is bending over backward, in the SD [the situation] must be likewise—as it is improbable to have complete<br>
silence after those noisy declarations about a crown in the eagle, etc. There are two<br>
possibilities:<br>
- a great coalition-us and the PUWP.<br>
- a small coalition-us, the ZSL and other smaller groups.

One of the elements of the situation is tremendous social impatience. Adam<br>
[Michnik] has had a sense of this impatience—[they say] so much is in your hands, and<br>
you don’t react.

The new configuration means a strong triumvirate, unusually tight. A strong<br>
Kiszczak, about whom there was talk here, is too strong. […]

We have to approach Kiszczak negatively. […] This is a configuration in which<br>
we have a minimal possibility of maneuver.

What can we do? Coalition with the ZSL is dangerous, as we cannot steer this<br>
process. A small coalition is on their good grace or the lack of it. In the end there are not<br>
too many of those contestants.

Only a great coalition is acceptable—a Government of National Salvation.

J. Kuron: That triangle is not a solution under any circumstance. Abstaining from<br>
voting—impossible, in any case we would lose the steering wheel, the Club would kick<br>
us out through the window.

The first variant: the strikes take off, which will start costing money. Anarchy will<br>
follow. Someone will have to bring stability. When a fire bursts, Jaruzelski will call on us<br>
to form a government.

With each day our situation is becoming increasingly difficult. Empty shelves are<br>
being played out against us, as it was in 1981. And our statements are in the Sejm.

If they [PUWP] are battered in the ZSL, SD—then in which groups do they find<br>
support? In the SD they are still trying to steer, but are saying that this cannot go on.

Stabilization is an illusion. If we remain passive, we will lose—then we will have<br>
to take it over in a worse situation and with less social confidence [then even currently<br>
exists]. As long as we don’t make a decision— we are not going to have a program.

Could it be a government of a great coalition? Initially it was supposed to be such<br>
a government: for us two, three ministries. What “Solidarity” has to give social<br>
confidence, less likely [perhaps the] possibility of obtaining a moratorium on debts.

The government should be ours, i.e. formed by us. We should vote against all of<br>
Kiszczak’s candidates.

H. Wujec: a PUWP government means a continuing crisis, waiting for a change.<br>
Now those price increases, people see it clearly. We are delaying solutions.<br>
The only chance is a broadly based Government of National Salvation. It would<br>
have to represent a new line, new spirit, have a different social perception. Can we do it?<br>
We have to search already for programs, people. We have to keep in mind that everything<br>
moves quickly.

J. Slisz: We need to form a government that is a great coalition—in which we<br>
should be the dominant force. How do we let the other side know that they should<br>
propose letting us have the position of prime minister? The coming 24 hours have to<br>
decide.

J. Stelmachowski: I agree with the diagnoses, but I don’t agree with the<br>
conclusions. The strategy is to wait until an auspicious moment. If the economic<br>
diagnosis is bad, it would be a folly to take over the government until such time as the<br>
“Solidarity” is the only way out. If we are expecting a deterioration [of the situation], we<br>
should not assume responsibility for it. They are not so weak and it’s not the parliament<br>
that decides. We need to be against Kiszczak; a strong PUWP government is not in our<br>
interest. It would be ill-perceived abroad—two generals in top positions. It was rightly<br>
pointed out as a jamming phenomenon. We should be voting against, but I would not<br>
vote against any candidate put forward by the General.

A. Michnik: I have been listening with some surprise to what the Senate Marshal<br>
was telling us. It’s something from the area of games, we don’t have time for it. I am<br>
afraid that in a little while we will have to leave that parliament, called off by people<br>
from the queues.

From my point of view, neither Kiszczak nor anyone else will change anything.<br>
This configuration is sentenced to death. Do you know what will be left of the PUWP—<br>
only trash will be left. There is a 60 percent probability that our talk is an academic<br>
discussion, but if Kiszczak doesn’t get through—I propose Mazowiecki, Stelmachowski<br>
and others. We have such an international constellation, a historical moment, when we<br>
can catch something. We should not use an argument that there is no program—as no one<br>
in the world has that recipe, e.g. what should Russia or Yugoslavia do?

We are doomed for one [program]—a sharp, sudden entrance into the market. To<br>
say this a year ago would have been a lot, we need to keep this in mind when we say that<br>
something is impossible.

There is no one who would defend a coalition with the ZSL. It’s falling apart. We<br>
are not attacking frontally, rather we propose something, e.g. Kwasniewski for vice<br>
premier, someone who will pull over the reformist elements.

O. Krzyzanowska: Tomorrow we need to vote almost ostentatiously. Our<br>
government will be in a much worse situation, as the Union is inclined to press demands<br>
and we will be calling for belt-tightening. If we don’t preserve the ethos of the Union and<br>
the opposition—the future election will be lost. Our hands are tied by the Union. Perhaps<br>
it will be our prime minister, but not our government.

K. Kozlowski: The situation is difficult, we should speak up strongly against<br>
Kiszczak and Sekula. Maybe in the end they will come up with something that will be<br>
acceptable and we will abstain from the vote. Perhaps in a few weeks they may<br>
desperately seize upon some combination, which will be acceptable. If they cannot come<br>
up with anything, then a government of National Salvation will appear to be a solution. If<br>
this happens, we will not join into a coalition but we salvage Poland: we then must have<br>
prime minister and demand tolerable names. A crisis situation, a Geremek or Lech<br>
government. The first thing that our new prime minister would have to do is to talk with<br>
the MON. History teaches that invasions, martial laws are threatening when the power<br>
structure is falling apart. We are close to this. I don’t know which general, but one of<br>
them will do it.

Tomorrow vote against [Kiszczak for prime minister], press ahead, see what can<br>
come out of it. Do not reject the option of a tolerable government, [if it is] partly a<br>
non-party one. Otherwise, press for hard terms into the government.

E. Wende: If this government fails the country, will there be an economic chance<br>
to get out of it? We must clearly say—no, it won’t be better. So, will our prime minister<br>
have better or worse chances of rescuing the country?

Z. Kuratowska: We have to vote against. Sekula doesn’t have a chance. We<br>
cannot wait any longer. What kind of professionals are they? It’s very hard to find them.<br>
Are we supposed to leave the country? The ovation at Powazki was a kind of an opinion<br>
poll[!] They were telling Brzezinski—we are ready to wait out this situation if you [the<br>
US government] are going to decide.

J. Slisz: In the corridor there are gentlemen from the ZSL and PUWP, they want<br>
to come here and talk.

(A brief consultation and the conclusion that this should not be discussed at the<br>
meeting. B. Geremek and A. Michnik are going for talks). [Recess]

B. Geremek: According to the latest news the situation is as follows:<br>
PUWP—12 against [Kiszczak ] (despite party discipline and threats)<br>
ZSL—21 against<br>
SD —?

It looks as though the solution is still that Kiszczak will form the government.

In justifying our position we will argue that we are against the continuation of the<br>
present rule. We are not in a position to extend credit to the teams which have been in<br>
power so far. We are accepting a diagnosis that under the present international situation<br>
our taking over the government is impossible. But potentially we are ready to do it.

A government of a great coalition came out of Jaruzelski’s mouth: “you are<br>
coming into our government.” If we are taking over, we form the government, we see in<br>
it a place for representatives of different social forces. It is a government formed by the<br>
opposition. It is an anti-nomenklatura government. That is how our position can be<br>
presented.

We reject a government [of] General Kiszczak plus Solidarity. If there is a chance<br>
to form a Government of National Salvation, which would have a chance of gaining<br>
public trust. If such a possibility doesn’t exist, then we will perform a controlling<br>
function to see that aspirations expressed in the election are met.

T. Mazowiecki: I don’t see a difference between the conceptions of government;<br>
from the general point of view each of them is a coalition government.

B. Geremek: It is a government formed by the “S” on the basis of a coalition. We<br>
are leaving the undemocratic system and the main problem is the structure of power.

A. Stelmachowski: It is the model that Hitler gave to Hindenburg—he just wanted<br>
the ministry of internal affairs and the chancellery.

T. Mazowiecki: This is a government proposed by us, but it still is a great<br>
coalition government.

B. Geremek: Lech Wa»imsa has two possibilities:<br>
- he will form that government<br>
- or someone else will.<br>
If we would get to the next stage (a 1 percent probability), if the president would<br>
talk with us, that is how I would present the proposal of Walesa’s government.

A. Balazs: We need to allow the possibility that they will form a government and<br>
wait for their overthrow. Within three months they will be completely finished in terms<br>
of propaganda. They are in the ultimate situation. This is a very difficult situation for us,<br>
too. We need to find some alternative solution.

B. Geremek: I told Kiszczak that his candidacy is not good, that someone else<br>
would be better. He has recognized this argument.

B. Geremek: The motion on an Extraordinary Commission has not passed. It has<br>
the backing of half of the ZSL, half of SD and a little in the PUWP, it has a chance of<br>
passage.

The following team will be needed:<br>
